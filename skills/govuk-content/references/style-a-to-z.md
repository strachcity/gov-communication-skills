# A to Z style rules

The GOV.UK A to Z style rules a review or draft needs most often. These apply to all channels unless a rule says otherwise.

Source: GOV.UK A to Z style guide, https://guidance.publishing.service.gov.uk/writing-to-gov-uk-standards/style-guides/a-to-z-style-guide/, unless a rule names another source.

Last checked: 6 October 2026

Lines starting with ">" are quoted from the source. Lines starting with "Interpretation:" are ours. If the live guidance differs from this file, the live guidance wins and this file is wrong until fixed.

The A to Z has hundreds of entries. This file holds a selection. For anything not here, check the live A to Z before deciding.

## Plain English and word choice

> Do not use formal or long words when easy or short ones will do. Use 'buy' instead of 'purchase', 'help' instead of 'assist', and 'about' instead of 'approximately'.

Source: "Use clear language", https://guidance.publishing.service.gov.uk/writing-to-gov-uk-standards/writing-guidelines/clear-language/

> Words ending in '–ion' and '–ment' tend to make sentences longer and more complicated than they need to be.

Interpretation: treat '-ion' and '-ment' words as suggestions to check, not errors.

### Words to avoid

> Plain English is mandatory for all of GOV.UK so avoid using these words

The list, with the suggested replacements:

- agenda (unless it's for a meeting): plan
- advance: improve, or something more specific
- collaborate: work with
- combat (unless military): solve, fix, or something more specific
- commit or pledge: "plan to x" or "we're going to x", where x is a specific verb
- counter: prevent, or rephrase as a solution
- deliver: make, create, provide, or a more specific term. Pizzas, post and services are delivered, not abstract concepts like improvements
- deploy (unless military or software): use, or build, create or put into place
- dialogue: spoke to, discussion
- disincentivise: discourage, deter
- empower: allow, give permission
- facilitate: say specifically how you're helping, like "run" for a workshop
- focus: work on, concentrate on
- foster (unless it's children): encourage, help
- impact (unless a collision): have an effect on, influence
- incentivise: encourage, motivate
- initiate: start, begin
- key (unless it unlocks something): usually not needed, or important, significant
- land (unless aircraft): get, achieve
- leverage (unless financial): influence, use
- liaise: work with, work alongside
- overarching: usually not needed, or encompassing
- progress: work on, develop, make progress
- promote (unless an ad campaign or career advancement): recommend, support
- robust (unless a sturdy object): well thought out, comprehensive
- slim down (unless a waistline): make smaller, reduce the size
- streamline: simplify, remove unnecessary administration
- strengthening (unless structures): increasing funding, concentrating on, adding more staff
- tackle (unless fishing or rugby): stop, solve, deal with
- transform: describe what you're doing to change the thing
- utilise: use

> Avoid using metaphors - they do not say what you actually mean and lead to slower comprehension of your content.

The metaphors it lists include:

- drive: create, cause, encourage
- drive out (unless cattle): stop, avoid, prevent
- going or moving forward: from now on, in the future
- in order to: usually not needed
- hub, portal or one-stop shop: website, service
- ring fencing: separate, or for budgets "money that will be spent on x"

### Other words

> Don't use "click" when talking about user interfaces because not all users click. Use "select".

"Right-click" is fine if the user needs to right-click.

> Use 'fine' instead of 'financial penalty'.

> Do not use FAQs on GOV.UK. If you write content by starting with user needs, you will not need to use FAQs.

"email" is one word. Do not use "email" to mean an email address.

## Contractions

> Avoid negative contractions like can't and don't. Many users find them harder to read, or misread them as the opposite of what they say. Use cannot, instead of can't.

> Avoid complex or conditional contractions such as should've, could've, would've too.

The "Use clear language" page adds "they've" to that list, and says to "Use contractions like 'you'll'".

## Requirements: must, need to, can

Source: "Use clear language", section "Make requirements clear".

> If you're talking about a legal requirement, use 'must'.

> If a requirement is legal, but it's administrative or part of a process that will not have criminal repercussions, then use 'need'.

> If something is optional, you can use 'can'. Avoid more complicated terms like 'You may be able to'.

Interpretation: "should" is not defined in this guidance. Common practice treats it as a recommendation. If "should" is used, check whether the writer means a requirement, and flag it if that is unclear.

Interpretation: changing "must" to "need to", or the other way round, can change the legal meaning. Flag it rather than changing it silently unless the content makes the right word obvious.

## Abbreviations

> The first time you use an abbreviation on a page, explain it in full. You can then refer to it by initials only on the rest of the page.

> Do not use full stops in abbreviations: BBC, not B.B.C.

The entry lists abbreviations that do not need explaining, including BBC, DVLA, EU, GP, ID, MOT, MP, NHS, PAYE, UK, URL, USA and VAT, plus file types like PDF.

> 'eg' can sometimes be read aloud as 'egg' by screen reading software. Instead use 'for example', 'such as', 'like' or 'including'

> 'etc' can usually be avoided.

For 'ie', the entry says to rewrite the sentence, or use 'meaning' or 'that is'.

On service pages, "Writing for user interfaces" also says to spell acronyms out in full on each page, and not rely on people remembering them across pages.

## Punctuation

> Do not use semicolons as they are often mis-read.

> Use 'and' rather than '&', unless it's a department's logo image or a company's name as it appears on the Companies House register.

> Do not use round brackets to refer to something that could either be singular or plural

Use the plural instead: "documents", not "document(s)".

> Use [square brackets] for explanatory notes in reported speech or for placeholder text

> One space after a full stop, not 2.

Hyphens:

> Hyphenate: re- words starting with e, like re-evaluate, co-ordinate, co-operate

> Do not hyphenate: reuse, reinvent, reorder, reopen, email

> Do not use a hyphen unless it's confusing without it

The entry suggests the Guardian style guide for other hyphenation questions.

Interpretation: the A to Z does not ban dashes outright. It says to use "to" in ranges, and the bullet points entry allows "commas or dashes to expand on an item". "No dashes at all" is a house rule some teams add, not a GOV.UK rule.

## Capitals

> DO NOT USE BLOCK CAPITALS FOR LARGE AMOUNTS OF TEXT AS IT'S QUITE HARD TO READ.

> Always use sentence case, even in page titles and service names.

Exceptions include proper nouns, specific departments, specific job titles, place names, brand names, titles of specific acts, and named government schemes known outside government. "Government", "minister" (unless part of a specific job title) and "department" (unless a specific one) are lower case.

## Numbers

> Use 'one' unless you're talking about a step, a date, a point in a list or another situation where using the numeral makes more sense

> Write all other numbers in numerals (including 2 to 9) except where it's part of a common expression like 'one or two of them' where numerals would look strange.

> If a number starts a sentence, write it out in full (Thirty-four, for example) except for dates or where it starts a title or subheading.

> For numerals over 999, insert a comma for clarity: 9,000.

> Write out and hyphenate fractions: two-thirds, three-quarters.

> Use a % sign for percentages: 50%.

> Use a 0 where there's no digit before the decimal point (for example, 0.5 not .5).

> Use '500 to 900' and not '500-900' (except in tables).

> Spell out first to ninth. After that use 10th, 11th and so on.

Ages:

> Use 'aged 4 to 16 years', not '4-16 years'.

> Avoid using 'the over 50s' or 'under-18s'. Instead, make it clear who's included: 'aged 50 years and over' and 'aged 17 and under'.

## Dates and times

> use upper case for months

> do not use ordinal numbers for the day - for example: 2 June and not 2nd June

> do not use a comma between the month and year - for example: 4 June 2017

> use 'to' in date ranges and not hyphens, en rules or em dashes

> when mentioning a deadline, use 'on or before' - for example, 'Submit the form on or before 6 September 2025'

Times:

> use 'to' in time ranges, not hyphens, en rules or em dashes: 10am to 11am (not 10-11am)

> 5:30pm (not 1730hrs)

> midnight (not 00:00)

> midday (not 12 noon, noon or 12pm)

> Midnight is the first minute of the day, not the last. You should consider using "11:59pm" to avoid confusion about a single, specific time.

Opening times go on separate lines for different days: "Monday to Friday, 9am to 5pm", then "Saturday, 10am to 1pm".

## Money

> Use the £ symbol: £75

> Do not use decimals unless pence are included: £75.50 but not £75.00

> Write out pence in full: calls will cost 4 pence per minute from a landline.

> Always use million in money (and billion): £138 million.

> Do not abbreviate million to m.

## Bullet points and steps

> you always use a lead-in line

> you use more than one bullet

> the bullets make sense running on from the lead-in line

> you use lower case at the start of the bullet

> you do not use more than one sentence per bullet - use commas or dashes to expand on an item

> you do not put 'or' or 'and' after the bullets

> you do not make the whole bullet a link if it's a long phrase

> you do not put a semicolon at the end of a bullet

> there is no full stop after the last bullet

The entry also warns that the examples in a list can make users think it is complete. Check whether a list is meant to be exhaustive.

Steps:

> Use numbered steps instead of bullet points to guide a user through a process. You do not need a lead-in line and you can use formatting like links and attachments in steps.

> Steps end in a full stop because each should be a complete sentence.

## Bold, italics and quotes

> Only use bold to indicate interface elements in text that are explicitly telling the user what to do

For example, "Select **Start**." Do not use bold for emphasis. To emphasise, front-load sentences, or use headings, bullet points or callouts.

GOV.UK Notify does not allow bold in emails, text messages or letters. See `channels.md`.

> Do not use italics. Use 'single quotation marks' if referring to a document, scheme or initiative.

Single quotes are for unusual terms users need to know (first mention only), quoted words like a 'Submit' button, publication titles and names of notifications. Double quotes are only for direct quotations.

## Links and email addresses

> Front-load your link text with the relevant terms and make them active and specific. Always link to online services first. Offer offline alternatives afterwards, when possible.

From "Add links": make link text descriptive, never "click here" or "more", do not reuse the same link text for different pages, and tell users if a link takes them off GOV.UK. If a link starts a task, start it with a verb.

> Write email addresses in full, in lower case and as active links. Do not include any other words in the link text.

Rules for links in emails, text messages and letters are in `channels.md`.

## Contact details

Telephone numbers:

> Before the number, add 'Telephone:' or 'Mobile phone:' – do not use 'Mobile:' or 'Mob:'.

Separate the area or mobile code from the rest of the number, like "020 7946 0457" or "07700 900 265".

UK addresses:

- start each part on a new line
- write the town and postcode on separate lines
- no commas at the end of lines
- put the country after the postcode, and only if the reader may be writing from abroad

Bank details:

> do not use a table - use bullet points and a lead-in line instead

> use spaces rather than hyphens in sort codes - 60 70 80 (not 60-70-80)

## People and inclusive language

> Address the user as 'you' where possible and avoid using gendered pronouns like 'he' and 'she'.

> If you do need to refer to gender, use 'women' and 'men' rather than 'males' and 'females'.

Use "disabled people", not "the disabled" or "people with disabilities". GOV.UK publishes a fuller list: "Inclusive language: words to use and avoid when writing about disability", https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/inclusive-communication/inclusive-language-words-to-use-and-avoid-when-writing-about-disability

> Refer to ethnic minority groups individually, rather than as a single group. Where it's absolutely necessary to group people from different ethnic minority backgrounds, use 'ethnic minorities' or 'people from ethnic minority backgrounds.'

Do not use BAME or BME.

Use "allow list" and "block list", not whitelist and blacklist.

## Organisations and "we"

> Use the singular verb form when referring to organisations by name. Use 'they' when replacing an organisation name with a pronoun.

> You should only use 'we' if it's clear which organisation you're referring to.

From "Use the right tone": in most content, use the organisation's full name before you use "we".

Interpretation: a reader may land part way down a web page from search, so check that the name appears in the same section before "we" is used.

> Use local council, instead of local authority, where possible.
