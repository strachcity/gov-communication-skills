## Data minimisation

UK GDPR Article 5(1)(c), quoted by the ICO:

> (c) adequate, relevant and limited to what is necessary in relation to the purposes for which they are processed (data minimisation)

The ICO explains this as:

> adequate – sufficient to properly fulfil your stated purpose;

> relevant – has a rational link to that purpose; and

> limited to what is necessary – you do not hold more than you need for that purpose.

Source: ICO, "Principle (c): Data minimisation"

Interpretation: for a message, the purpose is what the customer needs to know or do at that moment. Each piece of personal information in the message should be needed for that. Too little can also be a problem: "adequate" means the message must still do its job.

## Accuracy

UK GDPR Article 5(1)(d), quoted by the ICO:

> (d) accurate and, where necessary, kept up to date; every reasonable step must be taken to ensure that personal data that are inaccurate, having regard to the purposes for which they are processed, are erased or rectified without delay ('accuracy')

Source: ICO, "Principle (d): Accuracy"

Interpretation: an out-of-date phone number, email address or postal address means a message goes to someone else. The more a message reveals, the more this matters.

## Security

UK GDPR Article 5(1)(f), quoted by the ICO:

> 'Processed in a manner that ensures appropriate security of the personal data, including protection against unauthorised or unlawful processing and against accidental loss, destruction or damage, using appropriate technical or organisational measures'

> The UK GDPR does not define the security measures that you should have in place. It requires you to have a level of security that is 'appropriate' to the risks presented by your processing.

Source: ICO, "A guide to data security"

The ICO also says the fact of sending a message can reveal information:

> Even if email content doesn't have anything sensitive in it, showing which people receive an email could disclose confidential information about them. For example, sending information about the new opening hours of an HIV clinic would reveal the recipients are likely to use that service.

Source: ICO, "Data security advice", the section on using blind carbon copy

Interpretation: the same reasoning applies to a sender name, a subject line or a return address. If it shows what the message is about, it can reveal something about the person who receives it.

## Special category data

Health is special category data. The ICO says:

> where special category data is concerned, even stronger rules apply. This is because the special categories refer to personal information that could cause significant harm, such as discrimination or physical danger, if it was misused.

> If you're processing special category data, you should give particular consideration to how and why the data is used, and make sure you only use it when it's absolutely necessary. You should also take extra care to keep it safe. Generally speaking, the more sensitive the data, the more safeguards you need to have in place.

Source: ICO, "Data security advice", the section on special category data

The Government Security Classifications guidance for working at OFFICIAL says:

> Particular consideration should be given to Special Category Data, Criminal Offence Data and Childrens' Data, as defined by UK GDPR

Source: Government Security Classifications, "Guidance 1.1: Working at OFFICIAL"

Interpretation: a message can reveal health information without naming a condition. For example, saying a medical professional has been contacted, or using a sender name that names a medical team, lets someone infer it. Treat inference the same as stating it.

## Lawful basis

Processing personal data needs a lawful basis under Article 6. Special category data also needs a condition under Article 9. The ICO lists the conditions in "Data security advice", including:

> (h) You're processing the data for health or social care purposes (with a basis in law)

The Service Manual says:

> Your data protection expert or legal adviser will be able to advise you what legal basis to rely on.

Source: Service Manual, "Collecting personal information from users"

Interpretation: never choose or state a lawful basis or condition. Flag it for the DPO.

## Being clear with people about their information

From the Service Manual, "Collecting personal information from users":

> Use plain language to explain what personal information you're collecting and what you'll do with it.

> If you're doing something that has an especially significant consequence for the user, or it's something that the user might not expect to happen, do not rely on them reading the privacy notice to find out about it.

Interpretation: if a message tells the customer about something they would not expect, like their information being shared with someone, the message itself may need to say so. Flag it.

## Service messages and direct marketing

The Privacy and Electronic Communications Regulations (PECR) have extra rules for direct marketing by email and text. The ICO says service messages are not direct marketing:

> Direct marketing doesn't include 'service messages'. These are where you send a message to an individual for purely administrative or customer service purposes which doesn't contain any advertising or promotional material.

Its examples for the public sector include:

> appointment reminder or confirmation messages;

> acknowledgment messages such as confirming receipt of information or applications;

And:

> If you are a public authority and your messages are necessary for your task or function, these messages are not direct marketing, even if you decide to rely on consent rather than public task.

> There is a right for individuals to object if you are relying on the public task basis, even if your message is not direct marketing.

> If a service message has elements that are direct marketing then that message is considered direct marketing even if that is not its main purpose.

Source: ICO, "Direct marketing and the public sector"

Interpretation: messages about a person's own case are likely to be service messages. Adding promotion, like encouraging take-up of a different service, can change that. Flag any promotional content.

This also qualifies the Service Manual's 2017 statement "You don't need to ask permission to send transactional messages". The ICO guidance is newer and adds the right to object. Record both, and flag the right to object for the DPO.
