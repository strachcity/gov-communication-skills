# Service messages, optional messages and marketing

How to tell what kind of message something is, and what that means for whether it can be sent.

## Why consent is the wrong starting question

Interpretation: asking "do we need consent?" on its own leads to 2 mistakes:

- "we have consent, so we can send anything"
- "we don't have consent, so we cannot send an update about someone's application"

Neither follows. You generally don't need marketing-style opt-in to send a service message. You still need a lawful basis for processing, transparency, and to consider the person's rights and communication preferences.

## The 3 questions

Ask these separately for every message:

1. Is this a service message, or direct marketing?
2. What UK GDPR lawful basis supports processing the person's data for it?
3. Does the person have an objection or preference the service must consider?

This skill can help answer the first question from the ICO's guidance. The second and third are for the organisation's DPO. Flag them, don't decide them.

## What the ICO says

From "Direct marketing and the public sector":

> However the majority of communications that public authorities send to individuals are unlikely to constitute direct marketing.

> If you are a public authority and your messages are necessary for your task or function, these messages are not direct marketing, even if you decide to rely on consent rather than public task.

> If a message is not direct marketing (or is direct marketing but is sent by non-electronic means, ie by post), you don't need to comply with the marketing rules in PECR. But you must still comply with the UK GDPR (including fairness, transparency and the right to object).

> Direct marketing doesn't include 'service messages'. These are where you send a message to an individual for purely administrative or customer service purposes which doesn't contain any advertising or promotional material.

Its examples of public sector service messages are:

> appointment reminder or confirmation messages;

> acknowledgment messages such as confirming receipt of information or applications;

> messages about delays to payments or benefits or chasing debts;

> test result messages; and

> messages about changes to services.

On mixed messages:

> If a service message has elements that are direct marketing then that message is considered direct marketing even if that is not its main purpose.

On promotion:

> However, messages promoting services paid for by the user (eg leisure facilities) or fundraising do generally count as direct marketing.

On the right to object:

> There is a right for individuals to object if you are relying on the public task basis, even if your message is not direct marketing. Unless you can demonstrate "compelling legitimate grounds" to continue sending the messages.

The page doesn't cover feedback requests or surveys.

## 4 kinds of message

Interpretation, from the ICO guidance above. Needs confirmation by the organisation's DPO.

| Kind | Example | Consent before sending? | Other questions for the DPO |
|---|---|---|---|
| core service message | a receipt, a request for evidence, a decision | usually not needed just because it's an email or text | the lawful basis, transparency, and suitable contact details and channel |
| optional service update | proactive progress updates, convenience reminders | may not be needed if it's necessary and proportionate to the service's function, but preferences and objections matter | whether the service treats it as optional, and how a person can stop it |
| feedback or satisfaction request | "tell us about your experience" | depends on its purpose and lawful basis. Don't assume it's a service message | whether it's research or promotion, and whether taking part is genuinely optional |
| promotional message | promoting an unrelated or paid-for service, or fundraising | direct marketing rules may apply | PECR and UK GDPR |

Interpretation, for every kind:

- a service message stays a service message only if it contains no promotion. Flag any promotional content added to a case message
- a message can be sent without consent and still need a lawful basis, transparency, a suitable channel and respect for the person's rights
- a person may object to messages sent under public task. Whether the service must stop is for the DPO

## What this means for the patterns

- messages for case moments, like "We've received it" or "We need something from you", are likely to be core service messages
- a follow-up asking for feedback is a different kind of message, even when attached to a case message. Flag its classification
- if a service adds promotion to a case message, like encouraging take-up of another service, flag that it may become direct marketing
