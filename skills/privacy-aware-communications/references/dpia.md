# DPIA for communications

How to draft the communications part of a data protection impact assessment (DPIA), following the ICO's process.

Last checked: 6 October 2026

Source: ICO, "Data Protection Impact Assessments (DPIAs)" and "How do we do a DPIA?". The ICO publishes a sample template, which organisations can use or adapt.

> You don't have to use this template. You can make your own to suit your needs, or use an existing project-management method, as long as it covers all the key elements of the process.

The ICO says this guidance is under review following the Data (Use and Access) Act. Check the live pages before relying on it.

## What this skill produces

A draft, clearly marked:

```
DRAFT FOR DPO REVIEW. This is not a completed DPIA. It has not been reviewed or approved, and it does not record any decision.
```

The DPO and the accountable owner complete, decide and sign it. The skill never fills in a lawful basis, a condition for special category data, a risk acceptance or a sign-off. It leaves those as clearly marked gaps.

## Structure

Follow the ICO's 7 steps.

### Step 1: Whether a DPIA is needed

The ICO says:

> Ask your DPO for advice. If you have any major project that involves the use of personal data, it is good practice to do a DPIA.

> If you are in any doubt, we strongly recommend you do a DPIA.

The factors the ICO's "When do we need to do a DPIA?" page lists include "Sensitive data or data of a highly personal nature".

List which factors apply. Do not decide whether a DPIA is legally required. Say "For the DPO to decide".

### Step 2: Describe the processing

The ICO says the description:

> must include "the nature, scope, context and purposes of the processing".

For communications, fill this in from the disclosure inventory:

- **nature:** which messages are sent, by which channels, and who or what sends them, including any processor like GOV.UK Notify and its retention period
- **scope:** what personal information each message contains, including special category information and what can be inferred
- **context:** the relationship with the people receiving the messages, and what they would expect
- **purposes:** what each message is for, from the customer's point of view and the organisation's

### Step 3: Consultation

List who should be consulted: the DPO, information assurance, and the people receiving the messages, for example through user research. Do not record consultation that hasn't happened.

### Step 4: Necessity and proportionality

For each item in the inventory, ask whether it's needed for the message's purpose (data minimisation). Leave the lawful basis and the special category condition as gaps for the DPO.

### Step 5: Risks

The ICO says:

> To assess whether the risk is a high risk, you need to consider both the likelihood and severity of the possible harm.

List each risk with its source. Do not score likelihood or severity. That's for the DPO. Risks for communications that the sources support:

| Risk | Source |
|---|---|
| a message reaches the wrong person because contact details are out of date | ICO, "Principle (d): Accuracy" |
| a message, sender name or envelope reveals special category information to someone who sees it | ICO, "Data security advice" |
| a message is intercepted, because email and text messages have no end-to-end encryption | GOV.UK Notify, "Security features" |
| a voicemail or call discloses information to someone other than the person | HM Passport Office and HMRC published guidance |
| a fraudster imitates the message to collect personal information | Service Manual, "Planning and writing text messages and emails" |

### Step 6: Mitigations

For each risk, list mitigations from the sources, like moving detail to a more secure channel, removing an item that isn't needed, or neutral sender names. Mark each as "proposed".

### Step 7: Conclusion

Leave blank, with headings for:

- the DPO's advice
- whether the advice was accepted, and why
- residual risks, and whether they're accepted
- who signed it off, and when
